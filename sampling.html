Sampling and Reconstruction

Follow a signal through C/D and D/C conversion in time and in frequency, rebuild it from its samples, and see what goes wrong when the sampling rate is too low.

SIGNAL x(t)

1 · The sampling chain, in time and in frequency

Sampling is modeled as multiplication by an impulse train p(t). In frequency, this makes copies of X(jω) every ωs = 2π/Ts. The C/D converter turns the impulse weights into the sequence x[n] = x(nTs), whose DTFT is the same picture with Ω = ωTs. The D/C converter turns x[n] back into impulses, and a lowpass filter keeps one copy. If the copies do not overlap (ωs > 2ωM), the original comes back exactly.

\( x_p(t)=x(t)\displaystyle\sum_{n}\delta(t-nT_s)=\sum_n x(nT_s)\,\delta(t-nT_s)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad X_p(j\omega)=\frac{1}{T_s}\sum_{k}X\big(j(\omega-k\omega_s)\big) \)
The ideal choice is ωc = ωs/2. Any ωM < ωc < ωs − ωM also works when there is no aliasing.

2 · Reconstruction as a sum of sinc functions

With ωc = ωs/2, the impulse response of the reconstruction filter is h(t) = sinc(t/Ts) = sin(πt/Ts)/(πt/Ts). It equals 1 at t = 0 and 0 at every other sampling instant. Filtering the impulse train places one sinc at every sample, scaled by the sample value, and adds them up. Between samples, all the sincs contribute.

\( x_r(t)=\displaystyle\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} x[n]\,\mathrm{sinc}\!\left(\frac{t-nT_s}{T_s}\right),\qquad \mathrm{sinc}(u)=\frac{\sin(\pi u)}{\pi u} \)

3 · Practical reconstruction: time-limited sinc, zero-order hold, linear interpolation

The ideal sinc lasts forever, so it cannot be built. Replace it with a sinc of limited duration, hN(t), which equals sinc(t/Ts) for |t| < NTs and is zero outside. Every sample still gets its own (now time-limited) sinc. Because hN(kTs) is 1 for k = 0 and 0 for every other integer k, the reconstruction passes through every sample, for any N. Between the samples it differs from x(t). For a time t between mTs and (m+1)Ts, only the 2N sincs centered at n = m−N+1, …, m+N are nonzero, N at or before t and N after it. The zero-order hold keeps the last sample until the next one arrives, and linear interpolation joins consecutive samples with straight lines. Each method is a filter with its own impulse response h(t), shown below with its frequency response. Hover over the top plot to see which samples are used at that t.

\( h_N(t)=\begin{cases}\mathrm{sinc}\!\left(\dfrac{t}{T_s}\right), & |t|\lt NT_s\\[4pt] 0, & |t|\ge NT_s\end{cases}\qquad\qquad \hat{x}_N(t)=\displaystyle\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} x[n]\,h_N(t-nT_s) \)
\( \text{For } mT_s\le t<(m+1)T_s:\qquad \hat{x}_N(t)=\displaystyle\sum_{n=m-N+1}^{m+N} x[n]\,\mathrm{sinc}\!\left(\frac{t-nT_s}{T_s}\right) \)
\( \hat{x}_{\text{ZOH}}(t)=x[m],\qquad \hat{x}_{\text{lin}}(t)=x[m]+\dfrac{t-mT_s}{T_s}\big(x[m+1]-x[m]\big)\qquad\text{for } mT_s\le t<(m+1)T_s \)

4 · Aliasing

Sample a single sinusoid x(t) = cos(ω0t + φ) with the period Ts chosen above, and reconstruct it with the ideal filter (ωc = ωs/2). If ω0 > ωs/2, a copy of the spectrum lands in the passband at a lower frequency, and a different sinusoid, the alias, comes out. It passes through exactly the same samples.
